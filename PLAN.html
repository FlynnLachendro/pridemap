What this actually is

From zero. The real terms the team will use tomorrow, with the obvious bits spelled out.

Start here: proteins, and why anyone cares which ones are present

Every cell in your body is full of proteins. They are the molecules that do the actual work: digesting food, carrying oxygen, sending signals, building structure, copying DNA. Your DNA is the list of instructions for making about 20,000 different proteins, but a given cell only makes some of them, in some amounts, at some times. A liver cell and a brain cell have the same DNA but make very different sets of proteins. That is what makes them different.

So if you want to know what a tissue is doing, or what has gone wrong in a disease, a very direct question is: which proteins are in it right now, and how much of each? Most diseases involve proteins behaving differently (too much, too little, modified, missing), and most drugs work by sticking to a protein. The field that measures proteins this way is called proteomics. (Genomics measures the DNA instructions. Proteomics measures what was actually built from them.)

What "a protein of interest" means

A researcher usually isn't studying all 20,000 proteins. They have a hypothesis about one. Something like: "I think protein MFF controls how liver cells respond to insulin, and I think it gets switched on by phosphorylation." That protein is their protein of interest. Their questions are concrete: Is MFF actually present in liver? How much? Is it phosphorylated? Does that change in diabetic animals?

Answering that with their own experiment means animals, lab time, instrument time, and months. So before doing that, a sensible researcher asks: has anyone already measured the proteins in mouse liver and published the data? If so, they can download it and look for MFF in it. The field calls this reanalysis or data reuse. That's what Charlotte does, and that's why she searches PRIDE.

How you measure which proteins are in a sample

The standard method is mass spectrometry‑based proteomics. Every dataset in PRIDE came from some version of these steps:

0 · Get a sampleorganism · tissue · cell line
A piece of tissue (a mouse liver, a human tumour biopsy), a body fluid (blood plasma, urine), or cells grown in a dish (a cell line such as HeLa). The sample is from one organism (human, mouse, yeast…) and one tissue or cell type.
1 · Extract the proteinsPRIDE field: sampleProcessingProtocol
The proteins are inside the cells. You break the cells open (grind the tissue, add a detergent buffer so the cell membranes dissolve), then separate the proteins from everything else that spills out (DNA, fats, sugars). You end up with a tube of mixed proteins and nothing much else. That's "extraction".
2 · Cut the proteins into peptidesenzyme · trypsin
Whole proteins are too big and too varied to measure directly. So you add an enzyme that cuts every protein at predictable points into short chains called peptides (roughly 7 to 25 building blocks long). The enzyme is almost always trypsin, which cuts after two specific building blocks (lysine and arginine, written K and R). Because the cut points are predictable, each peptide can later be traced back to the protein it came from.
2b · Optional: enrich or fractionateenrichment · fractionation
Sometimes you only care about one kind of peptide, e.g. ones carrying a phosphate group (phosphorylation, the most common "on/off switch" on proteins). Then you enrich: pull those peptides out and throw the rest away. Or, to see deeper, you fractionate: split the mixture into several smaller tubes and measure each separately, so rare peptides aren't drowned out by common ones.
3 · Run the mass spectrometerLC‑MS/MS · instrument · DDA / DIA
The peptide mixture is pushed through a thin column (liquid chromatography, LC) that lets different peptides out at different times, so they don't all arrive at once. Each one flies into the mass spectrometer (brands/models: Q Exactive, Orbitrap Fusion Lumos, timsTOF…), which weighs it very precisely, then smashes it and weighs the pieces. That weight‑plus‑fragment‑weights pattern is a spectrum; it's a fingerprint of that peptide. One run produces tens of thousands of spectra and a 1 to 2 GB .raw file. The instrument has two main modes: DDA (it picks the strongest signals to smash, so weak/rare peptides get skipped) and DIA (it smashes everything in windows, so rare peptides are more likely to be seen).
4 · Search the spectraPRIDE field: dataProcessingProtocol · MaxQuant, Proteome Discoverer, FragPipe, DIA‑NN
Software takes every known protein of that organism (from a reference database such as UniProt), works out what peptides trypsin would cut it into and what their fingerprints would look like, and matches those predictions against the measured spectra. Every match says "this peptide was present, so this protein was present". The output is the thing everyone wants: a list of identified proteins, usually with how much of each. Often several thousand proteins per sample.
5 · Deposit everything in PRIDEdataset · PXD accession
Journals require the lab to upload the raw instrument files, the result tables, and a written description of the sample and the method to a public archive. PRIDE, run by EBI near Cambridge, is the biggest. Each upload is one dataset (PRIDE also calls it a "project") with an ID like PXD001357. There are 42,108 of them, with 500+ added every month.

So what is "in" a PRIDE dataset

  • The raw files from the instrument. Huge. We never download these.
  • The result files: the protein list the lab got.
  • Metadata: a title, a paragraph describing the study, a paragraph describing the sample prep (sampleProcessingProtocol), a paragraph describing the software search (dataProcessingProtocol), plus a few structured fields: organism, tissue, instrument, sometimes disease and quantification method, and usually a link to the published paper.

The metadata is small (about 6 KB per dataset) and is all we need. The paper's Methods section, which is often free to read on Europe PMC, adds the details the lab didn't bother to type into PRIDE.

Why the method decides what you can see

Here is the part that makes dataset choice hard. Two datasets can both be "mouse liver" and one can be useless for Charlotte's question, because the steps above decide which proteins are even detectable:

TermWhat it meansWhy it matters for picking a dataset
EnrichmentPulling out one class of peptide before the run. Phosphoproteomics = enriched for phosphorylated peptides. AP‑MS / IP = pulled down one protein and its partners.A phospho‑enriched dataset is excellent for phosphorylation sites and nearly blind to everything else.
DDA vs DIAData‑dependent acquisition: the instrument picks the most abundant peptides to fragment. Data‑independent: it fragments everything in windows.DDA misses low‑abundance proteins. DIA sees more of them. If your protein is rare, DIA is your friend.
Label‑free vs TMT / SILACHow quantities are measured. Label‑free compares runs directly. TMT tags samples with chemical labels and multiplexes them. SILAC labels via growth medium.Changes how you re‑analyse and what "amount" means. TMT ratios are relative, not absolute.
EnzymeWhat cut the proteins into peptides. Trypsin cuts after K and R. Lys‑C, chymotrypsin, Glu‑C cut elsewhere.A protein with few K/R residues gives trypsin almost nothing to find.
FractionationSplitting the sample into several runs (high‑pH reversed phase, SCX, gel bands).Fractionated = deeper = more likely to catch a rare protein.
InstrumentQ Exactive, Orbitrap Fusion Lumos, timsTOF…Newer and more sensitive instruments find more. A 2010 LTQ dataset is a different tier from a 2024 Astral.
Sample typeTissue, cell line, primary cells, plasma/serum, urine…Plasma is dominated by a few huge proteins (albumin). The same protein is 100× harder to see there than in tissue.

Most of that information is in the free‑text protocol paragraph or in the paper's Methods section, not in a field you can filter on. PRIDE's search box can't ask "only phospho‑enriched DIA datasets". That is the whole problem.

The picture

Orange datasets = the ones that can actually answer the question. Orange tags = the method details that decide fit. Today they live in prose, so PRIDE can't filter on them.

The two problems Charlotte wrote down

Finding

PRIDE's search is keyword matching over free text plus a few dropdown filters (organism, instrument). It misses datasets that fit and returns ones that don't. There is no filter for enrichment, acquisition mode, enzyme or labelling, because those aren't fields.

Choosing

Even with a shortlist of 20, you can't tell which one's sample prep and instrument settings give you a realistic chance of detecting your protein without opening each paper and reading the Methods section. Charlotte did exactly that, by hand.

What we build (one sentence)

A language model reads each dataset's protocol text and its paper's Methods section, emits a standard set of tags (organism, tissue, enrichment, DDA/DIA, labelling, enzyme, instrument…), and a map + search over those tags lets you ask "which datasets could actually detect protein X in tissue Y?" and see the evidence.

Field doesn't exist at all

Enzyme, labelling, acquisition mode (DDA vs DIA), enrichment (phospho, ubiquitin, glyco), fractionation, cell line, sample amount, number of proteins identified. All in the prose, or only in the paper.

Keyword search lies

  • Searching "DIA" in text hits 2,908 of 7,000 datasets. With word boundaries: 134. Via the structured field: 68. ("media", "dialysis", "immediately"…)
  • 43 datasets say "murine" in the text but the organism field doesn't say mouse. Search for mouse, miss them.
  • "Shotgun proteomics" (3,668) and "Bottom‑up proteomics" (1,152) are the same thing tagged two ways.

The stickers: our tag schema

Aligned with SDRF‑Proteomics (the official sample‑metadata standard that almost nobody fills in) so a bioinformatician recognises every field. Each tag carries a value, a confidence, the evidence quote, and the source (metadata or paper). The evidence is what makes the UI trustworthy.

What a user actually does

Ask

"I want to validate phosphorylated MFF in mouse liver. Which datasets give me the best shot?"

→ model turns that into filters: organism = mousetissue = liverenrichment = phosphoacquisition = DDA or DIA and ranks what's left by depth, recency, downloads.

Get back

PXD049957 · Phosphoproteomics of active AKT in liver · fits: phospho‑enriched, mouse liver, TiO₂, Q Exactive HF watch: TMT‑labelled, so ratios not absolute

Every "fits" and "watch" links to the sentence it came from. That's the trust layer PRIDE doesn't have.

Ahmed's idea: split PRIDE into chunks

Same tags, different view. Treemap: organism → sample type → enrichment → acquisition. Click a chunk to drill. The map shows at a glance that 40% of PRIDE is human, 14% mouse, and that phospho datasets in mouse liver are a few dozen boxes, not a few thousand. Finding becomes browsing.

The tagger, precisely

  • Input per dataset: title, description, both protocols, structured fields rendered as text, plus the Methods section if open access. ~3,500 tokens.
  • System prompt: the schema with allowed values, five worked examples (Charlotte and Juri write these Saturday 09:30), the rule "say null and low confidence rather than guess". Cached with cache_control.
  • Output: the JSON above, enforced via output_config.format with a strict schema. No regex, no retries for malformed JSON.
  • Cost: Sonnet 5 batch ≈ $0.0055 per dataset → 5,000 datasets ≈ $28, all 42k ≈ $230. Haiku 4.5 halves that. Decision: tag the 5,000 most‑downloaded human + mouse datasets tonight on Sonnet 5, run the rest overnight on Haiku if the Sonnet labels look good.
  • Eval (your Hamming background, use it): Charlotte + Juri hand‑label 50 datasets Saturday morning. Script reports per‑tag agreement. That number goes on the demo slide. If enrichment agreement < 85%, fix the prompt, re‑run those 50, repeat.
  • Note: Claude Max 20x covers Claude Code, not the API. Tagging needs an API key with credits. Budget $50.

The three screens

Ask

One text box. Below it the filter chips the model extracted (editable). Below that a ranked list: one‑line summary, green "fits" chips, amber "watch" chips, each chip expands to its evidence sentence. Link out to PRIDE and the paper.

Map

Treemap of all tagged datasets. Drill organism → sample type → enrichment → acquisition. Clicking a leaf drops you into Ask with those filters set. This is Ahmed's "PRIDE in chunks".

Dataset

The catalogue card: every tag with confidence and the quote it came from, the PRIDE fields it was derived from, download size, paper link. "Report a wrong tag" button writes to a corrections table, so the eval loop continues after the hack.

What the app is

The plain description to give anyone who asks "so what did you build?"

In one paragraph

PRIDE Map is a search engine over PRIDE, the public archive of mass‑spectrometry proteomics datasets, that understands how each dataset was made. We pulled the metadata for all 41,545 datasets through PRIDE's public API (titles, descriptions, free‑text sample and data‑processing protocols, organism, instrument, PubMed link; never the raw instrument files). For each dataset we also fetched the open‑access Methods section of its paper from Europe PMC where one exists. A language model (Claude Sonnet 5) reads both and fills in a fixed form of 14 tags: organism, sample type, tissue, cell line, disease, enrichment, DDA/DIA, labelling, enzyme, fractionation, instrument family, proteins identified, search engine, one‑line summary. Every tag comes with a confidence score, where it came from (PRIDE metadata, the paper, or inferred) and the exact sentence that justifies it. Those tags go into a database, and the app lets you search, browse and inspect them.

No embedding model is involved. The "AI" is one careful read of each dataset by an LLM with a strict output schema, plus one small LLM call per question to turn plain English into filters. Everything else is ordinary filtering and ranking over a SQLite table.

Where it lives

Codegithub.com/FlynnLachendro/pridemap. Private until Sunday morning, then public under MIT (hackathon rule). Every commit is from after Friday's kickoff.
RunningLocally: ./dev.sh starts the API on :8000 and the web app on :3000. Anyone can clone it and run it on the bundled 694‑dataset demo bundle in under two minutes, no API key needed.
DeployedNot yet. When we want a public URL: the web app goes to Vercel (one command), the API goes to Fly.io or Railway with the SQLite file, or stays on a laptop behind a Cloudflare tunnel for the demo. About 20 minutes of work; decide with the team.
StackPython scripts run with uv (crawl, fetch papers, tag, eval). SQLite with full‑text search as the only database. FastAPI for the API, with OpenAPI docs at /docs. Next.js 16 + Tailwind + shadcn for the UI. Claude Sonnet 5 with structured outputs for tagging and question parsing.

The remaining ~41,000 datasets are findable by free text but don't have tags yet. Tagging everything is one command and roughly $900 on Sonnet 5, or a few hundred on Haiku. The UI says "694 of 41,545 tagged" on every page so nobody is misled.

What you can do in it

Askthe home page
Type a question in plain English: "phosphorylated MFF in mouse liver, low abundance". Claude turns it into filter chips you can edit. Matching datasets come back ranked by fit, depth, recency and how often they've been reused. Each result shows green fits chips and amber watch chips ("DDA skips low‑abundance peptides", "TMT gives relative ratios"). Click any chip to see the sentence from the protocol or paper it came from. If nothing matches all your filters, it drops the least useful one, tells you which and why, and offers to put it back.
Map/map
A treemap of every tagged dataset, split by organism → sample type → enrichment → acquisition, or other orders. Click a block to zoom in, click a leaf to search exactly that slice. This is "PRIDE in chunks": seeing at a glance that mouse liver phospho datasets are a handful, not hundreds.
Dataset card/dataset/PXD…
One dataset in full: the one‑line summary, all 14 tags with confidence bars, source badges and quotes, the raw PRIDE fields, the paper link, and a "wrong?" button on every tag that records a correction for the next tagging round.
API/docs
Everything the UI does is a GET or POST: /search with filters, /ask with a question, /map, /dataset/{id}, and /export.sdrf.tsv which exports the tags in the community's SDRF‑Proteomics format. Any script or AI agent can use it without the UI. That's the brief's "endpoints for agentic control".
Evalgold/
A 50‑dataset gold set for two bioinformaticians to hand‑label, and a script that prints per‑tag agreement between humans and the model. That number is the honesty slide in the pitch.

What it is not

  • It doesn't download or reanalyse raw mass‑spec data. It tells you which dataset to download and reanalyse, and why.
  • It can't tell you whether protein X is in a dataset. Protein names aren't in the metadata. It tells you whether the dataset's method could have seen protein X.
  • It isn't a replacement for PRIDE. It's a better front door that links back to PRIDE and the paper on every result.

The loop, as the team described it

1 · FindAsk · Map
Question in plain words → filter chips → ranked datasets with "fits" and "watch" reasons and the sentence behind each. New today: facet counts down the side (click to narrow), a clear notice when a filter had to be dropped and why, and Claude doing the question parsing (3 s).
2 · ConfirmWorkflow
Hit + shortlist on candidates. The Workflow page writes a three‑sentence case for each one against your question, shows the tags that matter and what's on PRIDE, and you tick the ones you'll use. Human in charge, as Charlotte asked.
3 · Set up the searchReanalyse panel on each dataset
The files PRIDE holds (raw / peak lists / submitter results, sizes, links) and the database‑search parameters read from the tags: tool (MaxQuant/FragPipe for DDA, DIA‑NN for DIA), enzyme, fixed and variable modifications, quantification, FDR, FASTA. Each line says which tag and quote it came from. We can't run Mascot or MaxQuant (no API, desktop software); this is the set‑up sheet so the search matches the experiment.
4 · AnalyseAnalyse page
Upload the protein table (submitter's results, or your own MaxQuant/DIA‑NN output), pick group A and B, get log2 fold change, Welch t‑test, Benjamini‑Hochberg FDR and a volcano plot. Odetta's lyso‑IP pipeline runs as an option when the columns match. Her scripts live in analysis/.
5 · CheckEval page · Compare
Compare says whether two datasets are comparable before you run stats across them, and which method difference breaks it. Eval shows humans vs model on the gold set and five models vs each other.

Jargon, one line each

AccessionA dataset's ID, e.g. PXD007653.
Database searchSoftware turns spectra into a protein list by matching against a reference proteome. MaxQuant, Mascot, FragPipe (DDA); DIA‑NN, Spectronaut (DIA).
Fixed / variable modificationFixed: applied to every residue of a kind during prep (carbamidomethyl C). Variable: may or may not be there in biology (oxidation M, phospho STY). The search must be told which to look for.
Decoy search, FDRSearch a scrambled fake proteome too; hits to fakes estimate how many "real" hits are wrong. 1% FDR is the convention.
DDA vs DIADDA picks the strongest peptides to fragment, DIA fragments everything in windows. Different software, not comparable with each other.
Result tableThe search output: one row per protein, intensity per sample. What Odetta's scripts eat.
Full‑text indexPre‑built word list over the metadata prose. Finds words authors wrote; protein names are in the data, not the write‑up.
APIEvery screen is also a URL a script or agent can call; /docs describes them.
SQLiteThe database is one file on the laptop; no server to install.
